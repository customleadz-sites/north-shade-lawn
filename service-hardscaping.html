Overview

It all starts with the foundation.

Materials

Pavers, stone or concrete?

Most people come to us knowing they want a patio — not which material to build it out of. Here’s how we talk it through.

Pavers

Interlocking pavers are our most-requested surface, and they suit Michigan well. Because the surface is made of individual units, it flexes with the freeze-and-thaw cycle instead of cracking. If a paver ever gets stained or damaged, we lift that one piece and replace it — no patch line across the whole patio. They also come in the widest range of colors, shapes and laying patterns.

Natural stone

Irregular flagstone and cut stone give you a softer, more organic look, and no two installations are identical. It costs more in labor because every piece is fitted by hand, but if you want a patio or walkway that looks like it grew out of the property rather than being dropped onto it, nothing else comes close.

Concrete

A poured slab is the most economical way to cover a large area, and it makes sense for some driveways and utility spaces. The trade-off is that it’s one continuous surface — any ground movement shows up as a crack, and repairs are visible.

Retaining walls and steps

Walls do real structural work: holding back a slope, creating a level area, or turning an awkward grade into usable yard. We build with segmental block, natural stone and timber, and the engineering behind the wall — base, backfill and drainage — matters far more than the face you see.

We work in all three materials, so we have no reason to steer you toward one. We’ll tell you honestly which suits the space, the way you’ll use it, and the budget you have in mind.

Estimates

What affects the price.

Every hardscape is quoted after we’ve seen the site, because two patios of the same size can be very different jobs. These are the things that move the number.

1

Size and layout

Square footage is the starting point, but curves, corners and detailed patterns add cutting time on top of it.

2

Site access

Whether our equipment can reach the work area, or whether base material and pavers have to be moved by hand through a gate.

3

Excavation and base

How much material has to come out and how deep the base needs to go. This is invisible in the finished job and it is the single biggest factor in whether it lasts.

4

Drainage

Slopes, downspouts and low spots that have to be solved before anything gets laid.

5

Material choice

Pavers, natural stone and poured concrete sit at genuinely different price points.

6

Walls, steps and grade changes

Any change in elevation means more engineering, more block and more labor than a flat install.

Estimates are free. We’ll walk the space with you, talk through the material options, and put the whole thing in writing before anything starts. Request a free estimate.

Questions

Common questions.

How long does a paver patio take to build?

Most residential patios are a matter of days on site once we start — roughly a day for excavation and base, one to two for laying and cutting, then edging, joint sand and cleanup. Size, access and weather all move that, and we’ll give you a realistic window with your estimate.

Will it crack or sink over a Michigan winter?

Frost heave is exactly what ruins hardscaping in this climate, and it is the reason we spend so much of the job on what you can’t see. We excavate to depth, compact the base in layers, and build drainage in from the start. Built that way, a paver surface moves as a unit and settles back level. Built on a shallow base, it doesn’t.

Do you repair hardscaping you didn’t build?

Yes. Sunken patio sections, settled walkways and failing retaining walls are common calls. We’ll tell you straight whether you have a surface problem we can reset or a base problem that needs rebuilding — those are very different jobs and it isn’t fair to quote one as the other.

What does the 5-year warranty cover?

Our workmanship on the installation, for five years. Get in touch and we’ll go through exactly what that covers for your specific project.

Can you build hardscaping in the winter?

Hardscaping needs workable, unfrozen ground, so the build season runs roughly spring through late fall. Winter is a good time to plan the design and get on the schedule early — spring fills up fast.
